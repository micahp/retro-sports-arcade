Result

I read the shot, check and skating stride frame by frame, two sim steps apart, in each view. All three drew every action the same way, frame for frame, because they share one body and one set of poses. Wind-up, release, follow-through, check, stop and fall read clearly in A, B and C. None of them needed a 3D rig, skinning or retargeting.

Recommendation

  • Use the Gridiron sprite stack (B) as the main arcade renderer. It ran unchanged from Gridiron, turns smoothly at any angle and draws every player in one call.
  • Treat directional sprites (A) as an export of B, not a separate art pipeline. A is literally B baked into 8 facings. Use it where pure 2D or a strict pixel look matters more than smooth turns. Its memory grows fastest per action.
  • Keep low-poly 3D (C) as the pose-authoring and debug view. It needs no bake, so a new pose shows at once, and it's the bridge to R3 if that ever happens. It is the least retro of the three.
  • This picks a renderer, not a final look. Your eyes on the live panels are the real check.

Problems the frames showed

  • fixed During the wind-up the puck followed the raised blade off the ice. It now stays where the blade left it until release, as the arcade spec's possession rule requires. Re-checked frame by frame.
  • fixed A dropped stick sat at hand height during a fall, hidden by the body. It now lies on the ice.
  • fixed Skaters passed through each other at faceoffs. They now push apart.
  • open The reused Gridiron fall is a football fall. Its first half is upright with arms flung out, so a falling skater looks like he's standing without a stick. Hockey needs its own fall.
  • open Gridiron's body has no slot for a held prop except its football, so the stick needed an adapter hook (lab/hockey-adapter.js). Generalising that hook is the first change if the stack becomes shared studio tech.
  • open In A, turning steps every 45°. 16 facings would halve the step and double A's memory.
  • open No passing yet, and the nets draw as solid blocks.
